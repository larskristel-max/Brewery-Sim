Design Direction / Brewing Simulation Game

From garage brewer to living brewery floor.

A grounded, Sims-like brewery management simulation powered by real operational logic: recipes, tanks, pumps, fermentation, packaging, cashflow, quality, and consequences.

  • Isometric 3D
  • Sims-like workers
  • Real brewing logic
  • Progression from garage
  • Operational consequences
  • Minimal UI
Core Vision

The player should feel like they are managing a real brewery, not a spreadsheet.

Progression Arc

1

Garage Brewer
Small room, plastic fermenters, kettle, burner, hand bottling, low cash, unstable process. Gameplay is intimate and manual.

2

Serious Homebrewer
Better gear, temperature control, pumps, recipe iteration, local demand, basic branding, first recurring customers.

3

Nano Brewery
Stainless fermenters, kegging, ingredient inventory, scheduling problems, tank allocation, first staff member.

4

Professional Brewery
Full brewery floor: brewhouse, fermenters, brite tanks, packaging line, quality control, logistics, compliance and real cash pressure.

5

Advanced Operation
Multiple tanks, suppliers, exports, distributors, events, maintenance planning, automation, and operational optimization.

Visual Direction

  • Camera: Fixed or semi-fixed isometric camera. The floor layout must remain readable at all times.
  • Lighting: Warm industrial light, morning/evening atmosphere, practical lamps, steam, wet floor reflections.
  • World Detail: Hoses, pumps, grain bags, pallets, drains, control panels, steam, condensation, moving staff.
  • UI: World-space labels on tanks and machines. Panels only when needed. Avoid dashboard overload.
  • Workers: Sims-like autonomous brewers who walk, carry, clean, connect hoses, inspect tanks, package beer.
  • Tone: Grounded, tactile, warm, industrial, professional. Not cartoon craft-beer marketing.

Gameplay Loop

Plan
Choose recipe, inspect inventory, schedule brewday, reserve tank. Brew
Mash, boil, chill, transfer, pitch yeast. Ferment
Monitor temperature, gravity, yeast health, dry hop timing. Package
Bottle, keg, label, condition, handle losses. Sell
Local customers, bars, events, shops, online demand. Upgrade
Buy better gear, expand room, hire staff, automate processes. Repeat
Let constraints, mistakes, and quality outcomes create stories.

Realism Scale

Casual
Faster brewing, simplified fermentation, fewer failures, easier money, accessible to general players.
Realistic
Tank timing, ingredient variability, temperature control, contamination risk, oxygen exposure, staff mistakes.
Hardcore
Traceability, excise, HACCP, detailed losses, supplier delays, cashflow pressure, full operational realism.

Do / Don’t

Do
  • Make the brewery floor the main interface.
  • Use staff animations to show work.
  • Keep UI contextual and minimal.
  • Make mistakes visible in the world.
  • Let systems create stories.
Don’t
  • Do not make it a text dashboard with a brewery background.
  • Do not start with massive scope.
  • Do not overdo cartoon styling.
  • Do not separate simulation logic from Operon logic long term.
  • Do not add compliance before the core loop is fun.

Recommended Prototype

First playable prototype should be extremely small:

  • One garage room.
  • One player character or brewer.
  • One kettle.
  • One fermenter.
  • One recipe.
  • One packaging method.
  • One local customer/order.
  • One upgrade: buy a better fermenter or move to a bigger space.

The success test: Can the player brew → ferment → package → sell → upgrade and want to do it again?

Operon Connection

Long term, the game should not become a separate fake simulation. It should reuse the same operational concepts as Operon:

  • recipes
  • batches
  • ingredients
  • lots
  • tasks
  • tank states
  • packaging outputs
  • quality issues
  • inventory depletion

The difference is presentation: Operon is the professional control system; the game is the living simulation layer.
